### This week's case: Binance

Binance Holdings Limited pleaded guilty to violating the Bank Secrecy Act, failing to register as a money transmitting business, and violating US sanctions law. Its founder and chief executive, Changpeng Zhao, pleaded guilty to failing to maintain an effective anti-money laundering programme and resigned. Under the Justice Department resolution, Binance agreed to forfeit $2.51bn and pay a criminal fine of $1.81bn, a total of $4.32bn.

The Justice Department said that for years users could open an account with only an email address. Binance required identity checks from August 2021, but users who had not completed them could keep trading until May 2022. It did not systematically monitor transactions, and it never filed a suspicious activity report with FinCEN. Treasury found it failed to report well over 100,000 suspicious transactions, including ones linked to ransomware, darknet markets and terrorist groups.

On sanctions, Treasury found that between August 2017 and October 2022 Binance executed more than 1.67 million trades between US persons and users in sanctioned jurisdictions or blocked persons. Its blocks relied on IP addresses, and Treasury found that Binance suggested users could get around them with VPNs. The Justice Department said Binance caused more than $898m in trades between US users and users in Iran. FinCEN imposed a $3.4bn penalty and OFAC a $968m penalty, the largest in each agency's history, and Binance must accept independent monitors.

### Three checks

The Justice Department said Zhao understood Binance had to register with FinCEN and run an effective anti-money laundering programme. These checks are about testing whether a counterparty's controls work in practice:

- **Ask for evidence, not a policy.** A compliance statement says what should happen. Ask what screening is done at onboarding and what is reported, and how often.
- **Test controls that rely on one signal.** A block based only on location can be bypassed. Check what else confirms who and where a customer is.
- **Look at where the revenue comes from.** When the most valuable customers are the ones a control would remove, that control needs independent review.

### Bonus 1: a counterparty controls checklist

For each payment provider, exchange or platform you rely on:

1. **Onboarding:** what identity checks happen before an account can transact?
2. **Monitoring:** are transactions monitored, and who reviews the alerts?
3. **Reporting:** do they report suspicious activity, and will they confirm that they do?
4. **Sanctions:** how do they screen, and what besides location data do they use?
5. **Exceptions:** who can override a control, and is that logged?
6. **Evidence:** when did you last see proof, rather than a statement?

### Bonus 2: a 15-minute tabletop exercise

A fictional scenario to run with compliance, finance and operations.

- **Minute 0:** a news report says a platform you use for payments is under investigation for weak customer checks. Who in your organisation hears first, and what do they do?
- **Minute 5:** your records show payments to counterparties you cannot identify. Do you have grounds to pause the relationship, and who decides?
- **Minute 10:** a regulator asks what checks you made on the platform before using it. What evidence can you produce today?
- **Debrief:** write down the one decision you could not make in the room, and the person who should own it.

The checklist and tabletop exercise are my own practical suggestions, not drawn from the sources.
