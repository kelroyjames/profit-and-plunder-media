# Case study 1 (source: SFO release, 23 Feb 2026)

TITLE: Case study: AOG Technics and the check that worked
SUBTITLE: More than 60,000 engine parts, forged paperwork, and one question asked at source
VIDEO: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Jp5FhX5z85s

I have signed for a great many deliveries over the years, and the question at goods-in was nearly always the same: is the paperwork there? The AOG Technics case is a useful reminder of how little that question proves on its own.

## What happened
In 2023, an airline contacted Safran to check the authenticity of an engine part it had received from AOG Technics, a UK-based aircraft parts trader. Safran identified the certificate as a fake and alerted the authorities.

## How it was done
According to the Serious Fraud Office, AOG Technics sold more than 60,000 aircraft engine parts between January 2019 and July 2023, worth £6.9m and mostly for the CFM56 engine, accompanied by forged Authorised Release Certificates. Its director, Jose Alejandro Zamora Yrala, ran the operation from his home office in Surrey. He altered genuine certificates on his home computer, wrote false memos suggesting the parts had been bought from manufacturers such as Safran, and invented sales and quality managers who did not exist. The SFO says AOG made more than £7.7m over four years, 90% of it from fraud.

## What it cost
Once the fake was found, the UK Civil Aviation Authority, the US Federal Aviation Administration and the European Union Aviation Safety Agency issued safety alerts covering all AOG parts, and planes were grounded. The SFO estimates losses of more than £39.3m for airlines, including American Airlines and Ethiopian Airlines, and for manufacturers. In December 2025, Zamora Yrala pleaded guilty to fraudulent trading; in February 2026, at Southwark Crown Court, he was sentenced to four years and eight months.

## Three receiving checks
Every part arrived with paperwork, and a check that only confirms a certificate is present would have accepted it; however, one buyer asked the manufacturer whether the part was authentic, and the operation was halted. Three receiving checks follow from that:
- Verify at source, with the manufacturer or the issuer named on the paperwork, confirming the reference, part number and serial number.
- Test the trail behind the certificate, since a memo claiming a purchase from a manufacturer should be something that manufacturer can confirm.
- Approve suppliers on evidence, and ask each one where the part came from.

# Case study 2 (sources: NAO, June 2018; Commons BEIS and Work and Pensions Committees, May 2018)

TITLE: Case study: Carillion and the cost of dependency
SUBTITLE: There was monitoring, and there were ratings. The warning signs arrived with nowhere to go.
VIDEO: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Zqpd2R-oW7U

Supplier monitoring tends to be judged by whether it spots trouble early. Carillion is a case where the trouble was spotted, and the harder question was what anyone could do about it.

## What happened
Carillion went into liquidation in January 2018. It had around 43,000 employees, including 19,000 in the UK, and held about 420 contracts with the UK public sector. The Commons committees reported that it collapsed with liabilities of nearly £7bn and £29m in cash.

## The warnings
On 10 July 2017, Carillion announced a £845m reduction in the value of its contracts in a profit warning; by September, the figure had risen to £1,045m. The government had rated Carillion amber since February 2016 because of performance concerns. Even so, in the months after the July warning, Carillion announced £1.9bn of new government work, including two HS2 joint venture contracts worth £1.3bn that were approved before the warning and signed after it. According to the National Audit Office, none of the contracting authorities believed they had grounds to exclude Carillion.

## Down the chain
The committees found that Carillion enforced standard payment terms of 120 days, and that it owed around £2bn to its 30,000 suppliers, subcontractors and other short-term creditors. The NAO estimated the cost of the liquidation to taxpayers at £148m, while pension liabilities of £2.6bn were to be compensated through the Pension Protection Fund. Work stopped on some construction contracts, including two PFI hospitals. The committees' report summed up the company's rise and fall as a story of "recklessness, hubris and greed".

## Three supplier checks
Across central government, contingency planning was complete by 15 January 2018, the day Carillion collapsed. When one provider holds hundreds of contracts, the warning signs can arrive with few places to go. Three checks follow from that:
- Watch how your supplier pays its own suppliers; payment days stretching out can be an early warning sign.
- Map your dependency: which critical services sit with one provider, who else could step in, and how quickly.
- Build the exit plan before you need it, and test it.
