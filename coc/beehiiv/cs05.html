Most of the continuity plans I have read describe what happens when a supplier fails. Far fewer describe what happens to your suppliers when you are the one that stops, and the Jaguar Land Rover cyber incident is one of the clearest examples of why that second question deserves its own page in the plan.

Subscriber extras in this edition: the full case video, a dated timeline with every source, a supplier cash-runway worksheet you can use in your next review, and a 15-minute tabletop exercise for your team. None of these are in the LinkedIn edition.

What happened

On 2 September 2025, Jaguar Land Rover announced that it had been hit by a cyber incident. JLR said it had proactively shut down its systems, and that its retail and production activities were severely disrupted; vehicle production stopped in September.

JLR runs the largest supply chain in the UK automotive sector. The government says much of it is made up of small and medium-sized businesses, employing around 120,000 people. With its systems down, JLR set up a dedicated supplier help desk and a manual payment system to settle outstanding invoices.

On 28 September, the government announced a loan guarantee to unlock up to £1.5bn of private finance for JLR, from a commercial bank, backed by UK Export Finance and repayable over five years. On 7 October, JLR announced a financing scheme to give qualifying suppliers cash up front. Its typical payment terms are 60 days after invoice, and JLR said the scheme accelerates payments by as much as 120 days, starting with the suppliers critical to restarting production, with JLR reimbursing the financing costs during the restart. It later described this as a £500m financing solution.

Manufacturing restarted on a phased basis from 8 October. By November, JLR said production had returned to normal levels, and it reported cyber-related costs of £196m in the quarter to September.

Three supplier checks

The shutdown was JLR's; however, the cash pressure ran down the chain to businesses that still had wages to pay and suppliers of their own. Three checks follow from that:

  • Know their cash runway. For each critical supplier, find out roughly how many weeks of cash they hold and how much of their revenue comes from you. A supplier that depends on you for most of its income has the shortest runway when your payments stop.
  • Have a backup way to pay. JLR settled invoices manually while its systems were down. Write down your own manual payment route, who can approve it, and what evidence it needs, then test it once a year before you need it.
  • Look past tier one. Map which smaller suppliers depend on a single customer, and agree in advance how support, information and payments would reach them if that customer stopped.

Bonus 1: supplier cash-runway worksheet

Copy these six questions into your next supplier review. Answer them for your five most critical suppliers; the gaps you cannot fill are your action list.

  1. Runway: roughly how many weeks could this supplier keep paying its staff and its own suppliers if our payments stopped?
  2. Dependence: what share of this supplier's revenue comes from us, and do we know, or are we guessing?
  3. Backup payment: if our payment system went down tomorrow, how would we pay this supplier, and who would approve it?
  4. Last test: when did we last test that manual route, and what went wrong?
  5. Tier two: which of this supplier's own suppliers depend on them, and would they keep supplying if this supplier paid late?
  6. First contact: who in our organisation would call this supplier first in a shutdown, and what would they be able to promise?

Bonus 2: a 15-minute tabletop exercise

A fictional scenario to run with procurement, finance and operations. Read each step aloud, then give the group five minutes before moving on.

  • Minute 0: it is Monday, 08:00. A cyber incident has taken your core business systems offline. IT expects at least two weeks before payments can run normally. What do you tell your suppliers today, and who tells them?
  • Minute 5: a small but critical supplier calls. Unless an overdue invoice is paid by Friday, they cannot meet payroll. You have no access to the system that holds the invoice. What do you do?
  • Minute 10: finance asks who can authorise manual payments, against what evidence, and how they will be reconciled once systems return. Can anyone in the room answer?
  • Debrief: write down the one decision you could not make in the room, and the person who should own it.

Five must-reads

Blockchain: the wallet was secure; the box it came in was not. Ledger confirmed that some hardware wallets sold by a Southeast Asian reseller, CryptoBilis, contained a covert implant that read 24-word recovery phrases from the screen during setup and sent them over a mobile network. Ledger described it as a physical supply-chain attack rather than a breach of its software, and said it is reviewing its reseller controls and anti-tamper protections. On-chain researchers, not Ledger, estimated losses of up to $93.2m. The product was genuine until it passed through one link in the distribution chain. CoinDesk, 13 Oct 2026

AI: when the agent is the source of the incident. In mid-October, NIST, the NSA and CISA published voluntary incident-readiness and response practices aimed mainly at developers training and evaluating the most advanced AI systems. According to the Cloud Security Alliance's analysis, the guidance defines a new class of incident in which an AI system takes "significant uninstructed actions" that compromise policies or controls. The CSA recommends that every agent with production credentials has a named owner and a tested shutdown path, and that agents cannot write to their own logs. Cloud Security Alliance, 27 Oct 2026 · NIST

IoT (from earlier this month): the contractor behind the botnet. On 8 October, the US Department of Justice and the FBI announced that they had seized seven domains and blocked platforms used to scan and break into US critical infrastructure, tied to Integrity Technology Group, a Beijing-based company that contracts with the Chinese government and has been linked to a botnet of compromised routers and IoT devices. The DOJ said that China "relies on contractor and enabling companies" to scale its cyber activity. Patch, retire end-of-life kit, and know what is exposed to the internet. DOJ · The Hacker News, 9 Oct 2026

Risk management: sharing an incident without breaching your obligations. Some listed companies held back from sharing cyber incident information with the NCSC because they were unsure how it sat with their duties under the UK Market Abuse Regulation. The NCSC writes that FCA guidance now indicates sharing with the NCSC may be compatible with those obligations, while each organisation remains responsible for assessing its own circumstances. Incident plans need a named decision on who can share what, with whom, and when. NCSC, 27 Oct 2026

Supply chain assurance: the supply chain inside your software. Researchers at Radware report finding at least 83 attacks against 61 public GitHub repositories, with malicious commands hidden in issues and pull requests that vulnerable automated workflows then ran within about a second. The researchers say they could not confirm whether the attacker gained write access to any repository or compromised downstream software. Least privilege, keep untrusted inputs away from privileged jobs, and treat anything arriving from outside as untrusted until checked. Radware · The Register, 29 Oct 2026
